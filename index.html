The model

Next: the system

Every chip face is photographed under four lights. A scratch can show in one and vanish in the other three, so the model reads all four and learns which one to trust.

In October we tested it against the machine's own rule-based check, on 921 chips an operator had labelled by hand. The model was right 94.4% of the time. The machine was right 89.0%.

It caught 72 of the 85 bad chips; the machine caught 40. Whiskers, the thin metal hairs that grow off an electrode: 29 of 30, against 5.

One chip, 156 ms

The camera fires a trigger. My code finds that chip's eight photos, runs the model and writes OK or NG into the PLC before the pick arm gets there.

If anything doesn't add up, it writes nothing. A missing answer costs a second look; a wrong one sends a part to the wrong bin.

About

I'm Arnav. I went to school in Indore, studied computer science at VIT Vellore, and joined Tecdia India as an intern in January 2026. I stayed on full-time in July.

Most of my week goes into one system: a model that looks at chips coming off a line in Cebu and decides which ones are good. The model is the part people ask about. Most of the hours went into everything around it: talking to the machine's controller, collecting labelled photos from operators, and making sure it never answers for the wrong chip.

I like problems where the answer can be checked. My favourite bug so far was a measurement method that gave beautifully consistent numbers because it was measuring the gold pattern instead of the chip.

Before Tecdia I worked on a trading platform at Cygnus Capital, an ERP for Tecdia's office in Japan, and Alzheimer's detection research at IIT Jodhpur. I speak English and Hindi, and a little Japanese.
